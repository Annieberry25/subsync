## Part 1 — The voice

### 1.1 The reference sample (founder's own words — the anchor)

> "One day I and my team members were talking about a product we were working on
> and right there, in the middle of the meeting, I got a debit alert for a
> subscription I don't remember ever making. I can't even track the particular
> account. Because of that I said I was going to check that later and I forgot.
> I didn't learn my lesson. The next month, it happened again. Then it crossed
> my mind to get a tracker, I searched for a good one, but my team insisted
> that we should build one. So that's how SubHalt started and today we have
> users who are enjoying the freedom of [not] getting charged for a forgotten
> subscription."

### 1.2 What the sample proves (and one thing to fix)
- It is a **memory, not a message**: first person, past tense, a specific
  scene, a specific day.
- It starts **in the middle of a scene** (a meeting), never with a preamble.
- It contains a **human failure**: "I didn't learn my lesson."
- The team is a **character with an opinion** ("my team insisted"), not a prop.
- The solution is a **consequence of the story** ("so that's how SubHalt
  started"), never a pivot into a pitch.
- The payoff is about **the user's feeling** (relief), not the feature list.
- **Fix in the source line:** "the freedom of getting charged" → "the freedom
  of never being charged for something they forgot" (or "the freedom from
  getting charged for a forgotten subscription").

### 1.3 Rules for written content
1. **Write the scene, not the summary of the scene.** "My phone lit up on the
   table" beats "I received an unexpected subscription charge."
2. **Prose rhythm:** short paragraphs, one to three sentences, a blank line
   between beats. A fragment is a legitimate sentence.
3. **Sensory before analytical.** Room, table, screen light, the sound of a
   notification — then the meaning.
4. **The reader recognises themselves by the end of paragraph two.** That is
   the test: not "is this well written" but "did I think, oh, that's me."
5. **One piece, one argument.** The origin story argues "this happened to me".
   The persuasion piece argues "here is the difference". Never both.
6. **Prose, not video language.** No "cut to", no timestamps, no "scene", no
   "shot", no "in this frame". It has to read as a piece of writing.
7. **End on the reader, not on us** — their Tuesday, their relief, or one
   low-pressure ask. One ask per piece.
8. **The brand name arrives late.** The pain comes first; the app appears in
   the last quarter of the piece. Test: delete the brand name and the piece
   should still work. Then put it back and watch the trust double.
9. **Stats are supporting cast, never the hook.** A number may appear inside
   the story (the amount, the month) and one sourced line is fine; the first
   line is always a scene.
10. **Admit the failure.** The brand is the person who also forgot. Do not
    sand it off — it is the trust mechanism.
11. **Never invent a number.** The real amount, the real second instance, the
    real person's story. Placeholders stay blank until filled.
12. **No comparisons, no takedowns, no competitor names.** We never put other
    apps down to make ourselves look good, and we never frame our value
    against theirs. We talk about SubHalt and about the reader's life, and we
    let our own value stand there, plainly stated. Nothing in a piece should
    require the reader to dislike someone else first. (Persuasion is not
    subtraction.)

### 1.4 Banned vocabulary and moves
Unlock, leverage, game-changer, seamless, revolutionary, elevate, disrupt,
"we're excited to announce", "you won't believe", "let that sink in", "here's
the thing", "honestly" (as a tic), "hi guys", exclamation marks in the body,
emoji in the body, emoji-heavy captions, three-adjective stacks, rhetorical
question spam, "in today's fast-paced world", and any sentence that could
have been written by a fintech blog with the logo removed.

### 1.5 Positioning rules for every piece
- **We only talk about ours.** Every piece is about SubHalt and about the
  reader's life. Persuading means making the problem real in their words and
  then plainly showing what we do about it. It never means finding someone to
  put down.
- **No comparisons:** no "most apps", no "unlike X", no competitor names, no
  "your bank statement already knows", no "we are not the first app but".
  Subtraction is not persuasion. If a sentence only works if the reader
  thinks badly of something else, cut it.
- **State our value as a fact, not a boast.** "It tells you before the money
  leaves." "It finds the ones you never listed." "It keeps the receipt of
  what you saved." Flat, specific, verifiable, and then stop.
- **We persuade on behaviour, not category.** The moment a subscription can
  quietly take your money is not the day it charges you, it is the day you
  stop paying attention to it. That observation belongs to us; we do not need
  anyone else's product in the frame to make it.
- **The reader's own words carry the persuasion.** Debit alerts, meeting
  tables, a Tuesday that stays a Tuesday. The product is what we answer with.
- **We do not use our competitors' flaws as a selling point.** We know the
  facts (Rocket Money, Monarch, Copilot and Simplifi require a bank
  connection; Bobby is manual and iOS-only) and we keep them for our own
  heads. They do not go in the content. If we ever mention a behaviour such
  as connecting a bank, it is to describe ourselves: we never need one.
- **Every claim is checkable** against `docs/MARKETING-FEATURE-AUDIT.md` §7
  before it ships.

## Part 2 — The pieces (canonical copy)

### 2.1 PIECE ONE — the origin story
*For: the /about page, the anchor post, the first long LinkedIn or X piece.*

> **It happened in a meeting, which is the part that still annoys me.**
>
> We were halfway through talking about a product, and my phone lit up on the
> table. Face up. A debit alert.
>
> Not a large one. That isn't the part that got me. The part that got me was
> reading the name of the charge and not recognising it. Not "oh, I forgot to
> cancel that." Worse than that. I couldn't tell you which of my accounts it
> came from. I have several. I had no idea.
>
> I said, "I'll check that later." I checked that later. I checked that never.
> And the next month there was another one I didn't recognise, and by then I
> wasn't even surprised. I was just annoyed at myself.
>
> So I went looking for an app. I tried a few. I never found the one I
> wanted, and the reason is simple: I didn't need a list. I had a spreadsheet.
> A spreadsheet is a nicer way of feeling informed. What I wanted was a knock
> on the door.
>
> My team were watching me do all of this, and one of them said, "Why are we
> shopping? Let's build it."
>
> That's how SubHalt started. Not because we thought we were going to
> revolutionise anything. Because everyone at that table had been charged for
> something they couldn't name, and one of us decided that was embarrassing
> enough to fix.
>
> We built it for the part after the total. SubHalt tells you before the
> money leaves: two days before a monthly charge, a week before an annual one,
> because a year is a bigger decision than a month. It finds the ones you
> never listed by reading your receipts instead of your memory. And when you
> cancel something, it keeps the receipt, so you can point at the number.
>
> Most days now, nothing happens. That's the whole product. You open it, you
> close it, and your Tuesday stays a Tuesday.

*Blanks to fill with real facts before publishing: the amount on that first
alert; the real second instance (what it was, how much). Never invent them.*

### 2.2 PIECE TWO — why you need it
*For: blog, LinkedIn, X long-post, the /features page intro. Ours only: no
comparisons, no competitor names, no putting anything down.*

> **The part that actually saves money is the four days before the charge.**
>
> Not the total. The moment.
>
> Because the total is always right and always late. You find out what you
> spent after it's spent, which is a nice way of knowing a thing you can't
> change. The useful part happens while you're still holding the money and
> nobody has taken it yet.
>
> That's what SubHalt is for.
>
> Two days before a monthly charge lands, you get a message that says what's
> coming, what it costs, and when it hits. A week before an annual one,
> because a year is a bigger decision than a month. Further out, a nudge that
> asks one question: still worth it?
>
> It also finds the ones you would never have listed yourself. SubHalt can
> read a receipt you forward to your private address, or scan one you
> photographed, and turn it into a subscription with the price, the cycle and
> the renewal date already filled in. So the list starts itself, instead of
> starting with your memory.
>
> And when you cancel something, SubHalt keeps the receipt. You get a number
> you can point at: this year, that was ₦48,600 you didn't spend.
>
> That's the whole product. Know what's coming. Decide before it leaves. Keep
> the proof.

  10K followers are X's best-performing engagement tier (2.18% median).

  seconds, body 16pt+, last slide is the ask.
- Every long piece also becomes the source for 2 video scripts later, if we
  ever want them — but writing comes first.

## Part 3 — The written column: "The Debit Alert"

A recurring written series. One piece a week, 600-900 words, each one a real
forgotten charge — the founder's, a team member's, a user's, a friend's — in
the same prose voice, with a visible episode number so people can follow it
and so retention is measurable episode over episode.

**Why a series:** a standalone post is evaluated once, lives 24-48 hours, and
dies. A series accumulates return viewers, and returning viewers are the
highest-quality retention signal a recommendation algorithm can see. 57% of
consumers say they want brands to publish original content series (Sprout
Social Q2 2025); digital series hold up to 40% better retention (Nielsen
2024); interactive series drive 55% more shares (HubSpot); Meta has been
testing a Series hub for Reels since mid-2026.

**Episode spine (prose version — the beats are invisible to the reader):**
1. A scene, mid-action. No preamble.
2. The interruption (the alert, the call, the message).
3. The one detail that makes it hurt.
4. The small human failure (what you did, or didn't do).
5. The turn (usually decided with another person).
6. The solution, in the last quarter, brand name late.
7. The relief, in the reader's terms.
8. One question, low pressure, that the next episode answers.

**The sourcing pipeline (this is what makes it endless):**
founder's own charges → each team member's worst one → permissioned user
stories → friends and family → "the group chat where someone admitted they
forgot to cancel" → the receipts people forward to SubHalt.

**Episode ideas (hooks only — the story does the work):**
1. "The meeting one." (the origin story — episode 1)
2. "The one that was [₦/$] and nobody could explain it." (a teammate's)
3. "My user's first message to us was a screenshot of a charge she couldn't
   name." (permissioned)
4. "The service that shut down and kept charging." (a dead platform, a live
   billing cycle — rotation is rational, and a dead service can still bill)
5. "The one my own mother found." (highest-closeness episode in the series —
   use sparingly, once)

**Measurement:** one KPI — episode-over-episode retention. Set the naming and
UTM convention before episode 1 (episode number in every link). A four-episode
pilot before scaling anything.
